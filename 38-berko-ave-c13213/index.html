From the October 2019 purchase to today's midpoint is a gain of about $80,000 over seven years, before the cost of the 2019–2020 work. That is a smaller gain than the headline Hamilton numbers would suggest, and it is worth understanding why: you bought at the top of a renovation flip. Two markets are running at once and they are not moving together. Across the Greater Toronto Area, TRREB reported 5,040 sales in September against 5,592 a year earlier, with the average price down 5.0% to $1,006,409 and detached down 4.9% to $1,292,016. Buyers are waiting; sellers are listing less, with new listings off 14.3%.

Your pocket did something different. Of the twenty-five detached homes sold within 600 metres of you this year, the twenty-one that traded under $750,000 — the band your home sits in — averaged 99.6% of list and took a median of eighteen days. The quarterly averages in that band went $621,500, then $666,687, then $643,214. That is not a market in decline; it is a market holding its level while the million-dollar end of the GTA gives ground. Entry-level Hamilton is where the buyers who have been priced out still are, and it has behaved accordingly.

What has changed is volume. Five sales in June, five in July, two in August and one in September. Fewer homes are trading, but the ones that trade are still getting their price. For a seller that combination favours going to market priced correctly rather than waiting: the buyer pool is thinner, so a list price that invites them in matters more than it did in the spring.

Section 09 · Who buys a home like yours

Three buyers — and one of them is why this house is not an ordinary back split.

Of the twenty-five detached homes sold within 600 metres this year, only two had a finished separate-entrance lower unit with its own kitchen. Both sold quickly: 7 Berko Avenue in twenty-one days at 99% of list, and 50 Lawnhurst Drive, a three-plus-three raised bungalow, in eleven days. That is a thin sample, but it points the same way twice, and it is the thing about your home that the marketing has to carry.

The multi-generational family

The buyer this house was renovated for. Four bedrooms upstairs, a fifth and a full kitchen downstairs behind its own door. In Lawfield that buyer is usually a family bringing parents over or keeping an adult child at home while they save, and they are paying for the layout rather than the finishes. They will not care that the retrofit status is unwarranted, because nobody is signing a lease — but their lender might, so we price and position accordingly.

The first-time buyer priced out of Toronto

At $659,900 this is one of very few detached four-bedroom homes inside the Lincoln Alexander Parkway with four-car parking and nothing urgent to spend money on. The 2019–2020 mechanicals matter more to this buyer than to any other: furnace, windows, panel and hot water all done means no five-figure surprise in year one. They will be the most numerous group at an open house.

The small investor

For this buyer, the tenant in place is a feature rather than a problem, and the lower suite is the whole reason to look. They will underwrite on rent rather than on finishes, and they will ask about retrofit status and fire separation on the first call — so we should have the answer ready rather than discovering it during a conditional period. Expect this buyer to bid, but rarely to be the top bid against a family.

Section 10 · Worth a closer look

Four things to work through before anything is signed.

These are flags, not advice. Each one is work our in-house CPA practice can do with you as part of the listing rather than as a separate engagement billed afterwards — and each one is cheaper to settle now than to discover at closing.

A principal residence with a rented lower unitIf 38 Berko has been your home since 2019 and part of it has also been rented, the principal residence exemption may still cover the whole property. CRA's long-standing administrative position is that it can, where the rental use is ancillary to the main use as a home, there is no structural change to the property, and no capital cost allowance has been claimed against the rental portion. Claiming CCA is the step that usually breaks it, and it cannot be undone retroactively. The first question to answer is whether any CCA was ever claimed on the lower unit — before we talk about price.
The disposition has to be reported even if no tax is owingSince 2016, every sale of a principal residence must be reported on Schedule 3 with Form T2091 in the year of sale, whether or not the gain is fully exempt. Late-filing this carries a penalty, and it is one of the most common misses we see on a return that has otherwise been done properly. We would prepare it as part of the file.
Your adjusted cost base is larger than the purchase priceIf any part of the gain turns out to be taxable, the figure that matters is not the $587,500 on the transfer. Land transfer tax, legal fees on the 2019 purchase, and the capital portion of the 2019–2020 work — furnace, windows, electrical panel, tankless hot water, air conditioning, driveway — all add to the cost base. Those receipts are worth pulling together now while they are still findable, not in April. On the flipping rule, you are clear: that provision catches residential property held under 365 days, and you are seven years in.
The tenancy, and what it does to the saleNot a tax question, but it will shape the price more than any of the above. Under the Residential Tenancies Act a buyer cannot simply take possession: vacant possession has to be arranged before closing, either by agreement with the tenant or through the purchaser's own-use route, and the timelines are not short. Selling with the tenancy in place is perfectly workable — it just narrows the buyer pool to investors and families willing to wait, and that narrowing has a price. We should decide which way we are going before the listing goes live, not after an offer arrives.

Questions sellers ask

The things most people want to ask, answered before you have to.

What if it doesn't sell?

Then we adjust on evidence, not nerves. You get showing and traffic data weekly, and we agree in advance what a slow first two weeks means. Most homes that stall were mispriced at launch or shown badly — both of which we handle before day one.

Why not just list high and see what happens?

Because the market reads it, and this pocket proves it. 38 Coral Drive opened at $699,000 and took ninety-eight days and $49,000 in reductions. 19 Gildea Street opened at $749,900 and took ninety-eight days to reach $650,000. Meanwhile 217 Lawnhurst Court opened at $700,000 and sold over asking in twenty days, and 181 Bellingham Drive sold at full price in four. The first ten days carry the most buyer attention you will ever get.

Does the tenant have to leave before we sell?

No — but it changes who buys. You can sell with the tenancy in place, which brings investors and patient families, or arrange vacant possession first, which opens the listing to everyone. The Residential Tenancies Act sets the process and the notice periods either way, and our legal counsel walks you through it before we list rather than after an offer lands.

Our basement unit isn't a legal second suite. Is that a problem?

It is a disclosure item, not a deal-breaker. 7 Berko Avenue sold in twenty-one days at 99% of list with exactly the same caveat on its listing. What matters is that we say it plainly up front and price accordingly, so it never becomes a renegotiation during a conditional period.

Can I just do it myself, or use a discount brokerage?

You can. What you would be taking on is the preparation, the photography, the ad spend, the showings, the negotiation and the paperwork — and carrying the risk on all of it. Our packages exist so you can choose how much of that you want handled.

How long does it actually take?

From the decision to list, about two weeks of preparation, then a firm sale typically in thirty to sixty days. In this pocket the median was eighteen days this year. Closing is usually thirty to ninety days after that, and the date is negotiable to suit you.

What will I actually walk away with?

Use the slider in the net proceeds section — those are your real numbers at any price. The only thing not shown is your mortgage payout, which your lender confirms in writing.

Do I pay anything if we don't sell?

No. Commission is earned on a completed sale. Staging, painting and photography at the Signature and Concierge levels are ours to carry.

Should I renovate first?

Not here. Your mechanicals are done and your interior is seven years old, which is exactly what this buyer wants. Paint, decluttering and staging return far more per dollar than a kitchen does, and they take two weeks rather than three months.

What happens to my taxes?

That is the question most agents cannot answer. Terrence Marshall on our team is a CPA, so the tax picture gets reviewed as part of the file — the principal residence position on a home with a rented lower unit, the cost base, the Schedule 3 reporting — before you sign, not after closing.

Who will I actually be dealing with?

Me. I run the file from the first conversation through closing. Behind me is a five-agent team plus in-house legal and accounting, so nothing sits because someone is away — but you are never handed off.

What if I change my mind?

Tell me. We would rather pause a listing than push a family into a sale they are not ready for. This business was built on families who came back and who sent their relatives.
